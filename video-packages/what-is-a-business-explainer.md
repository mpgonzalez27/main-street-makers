## Learning Goal

By the end, the learner will understand:

- A business is a planned way to help people with a product or service.
- A good business starts by noticing a real need.
- A business must think about customers, price, money in, money out, and profit.

Source note: The SBA says a business plan should explain what a company offers, who its customers are, how the business will attract customers, and how it will make money: https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/write-your-business-plan

## Real-World Example

Business or situation: Nora’s Bookmark Basket

Customer: Children at a homeschool book club

Need: Readers keep losing their place in chapter books.

Offer: Handmade bookmarks with bright ribbon tabs.

Choice or mini-problem: Nora must choose a fair price and remember her supply costs.

Why this example works:

- It is wholesome and sheltered.
- It uses no modern brands.
- It shows a small product business clearly.
- It lets children see need, offer, customer, price, expense, revenue, and profit.

## Key Idea

A business helps people by offering something useful, then keeps track of the money it earns and spends.

## Script

Imagine you are at a cozy homeschool book club.

Children are sitting with chapter books. Some books are open on laps. Some are stacked in a basket. One reader keeps flipping pages back and forth.

She says, “Oh no. I lost my place again.”

Nora notices.

That is where a business idea can begin.

A business starts when someone notices a need.

A need is something that would make life better, easier, cleaner, safer, faster, or more joyful.

In this story, the need is simple. Readers need a way to save their place in a book.

Nora thinks, “I could make bookmarks.”

That is her offer.

An offer is what a business gives to a customer. It might be a product. A product is a thing people can buy. It might be a service. A service is a helpful job people can buy.

Nora’s bookmarks are products.

She calls her little business Nora’s Bookmark Basket.

Now Nora has to think like a founder.

A founder is a person who starts something.

First, Nora thinks about her customers. Her customers are the people who may need or want her bookmarks.

She does not say, “Everyone in the whole world is my customer.”

That would be too big.

She starts small.

Her first customers are children at book club who read chapter books.

Next, Nora thinks about supplies.

She needs cardstock, ribbon, glue, and colored pencils.

Supplies cost money before she sells anything.

That money is called an expense.

An expense is money the business spends.

Nora spends four coins on supplies.

Then she makes eight bookmarks.

Now she has to choose a price.

A price is the amount a customer pays.

If the price is too high, customers may say, “No thank you.”

If the price is too low, Nora may not have enough money to replace her supplies.

Nora chooses one coin for each bookmark.

At book club, five children buy bookmarks.

Nora earns five coins.

Money that comes into the business is called revenue.

But revenue is not the same as profit.

Profit is what is left after expenses.

Nora earned five coins. She spent four coins on supplies.

Five coins in minus four coins out leaves one coin.

That one coin is profit.

Now Nora can make a wise choice.

She could save the coin.

She could buy more ribbon.

She could make better signs.

She could ask customers what colors they like best.

That is market research. Market research means learning what customers need before you make more.

The SBA teaches grown-up business owners to think about customers, costs, and how a business will make money. We can learn the same idea in a kid-sized way.

So, what is a business?

A business is not just a money machine.

A business is a planned way to help people.

It notices a need.

It makes an offer.

It serves customers.

It keeps records.

It learns from what happens.

And step by step, it asks, “Is this helping people? Is this working? What should I do next?”

Founder Notebook question:

What need could your pretend business help solve?

## Narration Direction

Voice: Warm adult teacher voice. Friendly, gentle, curious, and clear.

Preferred narrator: Use the same studio narration family as the Main Street Makers workbook reader. Sulafat is the current Atlas-style narrator for “What Is a Business?”

Pace: Slow enough for ages 8–9. Leave small pauses after definitions and the coin math.

Energy: Encouraging, not silly. Smile in the voice, but do not overact.

Pronunciation notes:

- Say “revenue” slowly the first time: rev-uh-new.
- Say “expense” clearly.
- Say “profit” with emphasis during the equation.

Chunking plan:

- Chunk 1: Scenes 1–4, hook and need.
- Chunk 2: Scenes 5–8, offer, customer, expense, price.
- Chunk 3: Scenes 9–12, revenue, profit, choices, notebook prompt.

## Parent Note

This video helps children see a business as service first. The child learns that a founder notices a need, offers a product or service, thinks about customers, chooses a price, and keeps track of money in and money out.

Discussion question:

- What need does Nora notice before she starts making bookmarks?

Optional activity:

- Ask your child to choose one pretend business idea and write one customer need it could help solve.
